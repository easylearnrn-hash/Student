NCLEX Prep

Legal, Ethical & Safety Rules Every NCLEX Student Must Know

Client Rights + Client Safety + Nursing Accountability solve most legal, ethical, and safety questions.

Core Concept: Many NCLEX questions aren't asking "what disease does the client have?" — they're asking what the nurse is legally, ethically, and safely required to do.
Golden Rule: Protect the client while respecting the client's rights.
1. The Four Basic Ethical Principles

Autonomy

The client's right to make personal healthcare decisions — accept, refuse, ask questions, change their mind — even if the nurse disagrees.

Beneficence

Doing what benefits the client (e.g., providing pain relief to a client who is suffering).

Nonmaleficence

Do no harm (e.g., questioning a prescription that could seriously harm the client).

Justice

Treating clients fairly — care is never based on wealth, status, race, religion, lifestyle, or personal preference.

2. Other Important Ethical Principles

Fidelity

Keeping professional commitments — if you say "I'll return in 15 minutes," make a reasonable effort to do so.

Veracity

Telling the truth — never intentionally deceive the client.

Accountability

Taking responsibility for your actions, including appropriate nursing care and safe delegation.

3. Refusal of Treatment

A competent adult may refuse medication, surgery, blood products, procedures, diagnostic testing, nutrition, or other treatments. The nurse should not threaten, force, trick, hide medication in food, ask family to override the decision, or shame the client.

First response to refusal: explore the reason — don't assume it's about side effects, fear, cost, beliefs, or misunderstanding.

4. Competent Client vs. Family Wishes & Decision-Making Capacity

If a competent client refuses and family says "do it anyway," the client's decision generally takes priority — family members do not automatically override a competent adult.

Decision-making capacity means the client can understand information, appreciate consequences, reason about choices, and communicate a decision. Don't assume a client lacks capacity just because they're older, have a psychiatric diagnosis, or make a decision the nurse dislikes.

5. Informed Consent

Informed consent means the client has enough information to make a voluntary decision: nature of the procedure, purpose, risks, benefits, alternatives, and the chance to ask questions.

  • The provider performing the procedure explains risks/benefits/alternatives — not the nurse.
  • The nurse may witness the signature, verify the client appears to consent voluntarily and appears capable, notify the provider of unanswered questions, and document per policy.
  • If the client says "I still don't understand" right before signing — stop the process and notify the provider to clarify. Don't explain it yourself or just get the signature.

7. Confidentiality
  • Need to know: only access or share what's necessary for your role in care — not because a client is famous, a friend, or you're curious.
  • A family member asking "what did the biopsy show?" does not automatically get an answer — consider whether the client permits disclosure.
  • Never discuss clients in elevators, cafeterias, hallways, or other public/social settings, even without names.
  • Never post identifiable client information on social media, even with the name removed.
8. Mandatory Reporting

Suspected child abuse, elder/dependent-adult abuse, certain communicable diseases, and certain injuries may require reporting by law. When reporting is legally required, confidentiality does not prevent it. The nurse doesn't need to personally prove abuse — recognize reasonable suspicion and follow reporting requirements, without accusing the caregiver or delaying the report.

For intimate partner violence: prioritize client safety, private assessment, nonjudgmental communication, resources, and applicable reporting — don't force a competent adult to leave a relationship. If a client makes a credible threat to harm self or others, safety overrides routine confidentiality.

9. Advance Directives & DNR
  • Advance directives (living will, healthcare proxy, durable power of attorney) express the client's wishes if they become unable to decide.
  • A healthcare proxy/surrogate represents the client's wishes and values — not their own personal preference.
  • If the client currently has decision-making capacity, the client's current decision governs — it doesn't get overridden by an old directive or a surrogate.
  • DNR means do not begin CPR during cardiac/respiratory arrest. It does NOT mean stop medications, feeding, oxygen, hygiene, or pain treatment. DNR ≠ "do not treat."
10. Client Advocacy & Unsafe Prescriptions

Advocacy means protecting the client's rights, safety, preferences, and access to care — questioning an unsafe prescription, ensuring consent questions are answered, protecting confidentiality, respecting refusal, and escalating concerns.

Never carry out a prescription that appears clearly unsafe, incorrect, contraindicated, or harmful — clarify first. A provider's order does not eliminate nursing accountability, and "the doctor told me to" is not a defense.

11. Chain of Command

If a safety concern is dismissed and the client remains at risk, continue escalating through the chain of command until it's addressed. Use it for client safety, unresolved clinical concerns, and ethical/legal issues — not for personal conflicts like "I don't like the provider."

13. Documentation Rules

Documentation must be accurate, objective, timely, complete, and factual. "Client crying, states 'my pain is 8 out of 10'" is objective; "client is being dramatic" is judgmental — avoid the latter.

  • Never chart an intervention before it actually occurs.
  • Late entries must follow facility policy — never falsify the original time.
  • Never erase or destroy records to hide an error; corrections follow institutional policy.

15. Restraints & Seclusion

Restraints are never used for staff convenience, punishment, or routine fall prevention. Try less restrictive measures first: reorientation, decreasing stimulation, addressing pain, toileting, family presence, observation, environmental changes, moving closer to the nursing station.

  • Restraints may be considered only when the client is an immediate danger and less restrictive measures are ineffective or inappropriate.
  • A restrained client still needs monitoring, circulation checks, skin assessment, positioning, hydration/nutrition, toileting, and reassessment of continued need.
  • Never tie a restraint to a movable side rail — secure to the approved bed-frame location.
  • Using all four side rails to keep a client in bed can itself function as a restraint.
  • Seclusion is also a restrictive intervention and follows the same least-restrictive principle.

17. Scope of Practice & Delegation Accountability

The nurse only performs activities permitted by law, scope, policy, and personal competence. Delegating a task doesn't remove RN responsibility — the RN remains accountable for appropriate delegation, clear instructions, supervision, and follow-up. Never delegate clinical judgment: initial assessment, initial teaching, evaluation, care planning, unstable clients, and complex judgment stay with the RN. A UAP reports data ("BP is 78/42"); the RN interprets and assesses.

18. Abandonment & Safe Staffing

Abandonment means improperly ending responsibility for an assigned client without ensuring continuation of care (e.g., leaving mid-shift without proper handoff). Concerns about an assignment should be raised before accepting it whenever possible — once accepted, leaving without handoff can create abandonment concerns. If an assignment feels unsafe: communicate the concern, use chain of command, follow policy, protect clients, and document — don't simply walk out.

19. Impaired Healthcare Workers

Signs may include slurred speech, smell of alcohol, or unsafe behavior. Priority: protect clients from unsafe care and follow facility reporting procedures. Never hide medication discrepancies, ignore obvious impairment, or allow unsafe care to continue — client safety comes first.

20. Client Identification & Medication/Blood Safety

21. Ethical Conflicts, Culture & Blood Refusal

If a nurse's personal beliefs conflict with a client's lawful decision, the nurse must still provide safe, nonabandoning care — no judgment, delayed care, or coercion. Respect cultural/religious beliefs individually; don't assume everyone from a culture believes the same thing. A competent adult may refuse blood products for religious or personal reasons — respect the refusal, notify providers, discuss alternatives, and never secretly administer blood.

22. Client Rights, Right to Know & Communication
  • Common client rights: privacy, confidentiality, information, participation in decisions, refusal, respectful care, pain management, access to care, freedom from unnecessary restraint.
  • Do not hide diagnoses from a capable client just because family says "don't tell them."
  • Use qualified interpreter resources — never young children, random visitors, or unqualified staff for complex medical interpretation.
  • A client saying "yes" doesn't guarantee comprehension — use teach-back when appropriate.
23. Safety Reporting vs. Punishment

Safety systems exist to identify and correct problems. When an error occurs, protect the client first — the priority is not "who should be punished?"

Practice Walkthroughs

Practice 1 — Consent

A client scheduled for surgery says, "I signed the form, but I still don't understand what the surgeon is going to do." What should the nurse do?

  • A. Explain all surgical risks independently
  • B. Tell the client it is too late to change their mind
  • C. Notify the surgeon/provider that the client needs further explanation
  • D. Give a sedative so the client will be less anxious

Rationale: The client does not yet demonstrate adequate understanding — the provider responsible for the procedure should clarify it.

Practice 2 — Refusal

A competent client refuses a blood transfusion. The family requests that the nurse administer it anyway. What should the nurse do?

  • A. Follow the family's request
  • B. Respect the client's refusal
  • C. Administer the blood secretly
  • D. Restrain the client

Rationale: Competent client autonomy comes first.

Practice 3 — Medication Error

The nurse realizes that the wrong dose of medication was administered. What should the nurse do first?

  • A. Complete an incident report
  • B. Assess the client
  • C. Call risk management
  • D. Tell the next shift

Rationale: Client safety before paperwork.

Practice 4 — Unsafe Order

The nurse receives a prescription that appears potentially dangerous for the client's current condition. What should the nurse do?

  • A. Administer it because the provider ordered it
  • B. Clarify the prescription before administering
  • C. Ask the UAP to administer it
  • D. Ignore the order without telling anyone

Rationale: The nurse must protect the client and clarify unsafe prescriptions.

Practice 5 — Chain of Command

The nurse reports a serious change in a client's condition to the provider. The provider takes no action, and the client remains at risk. What should the nurse do?

  • A. Document and wait until tomorrow
  • B. Continue through the appropriate chain of command
  • C. Ask the family to solve the problem
  • D. Ignore the situation

Rationale: Continue advocacy when the safety problem remains unresolved.

Practice 6 — Confidentiality

A nurse sees that a famous actor has been admitted to another unit. What should the nurse do?

  • A. Open the chart to see the diagnosis
  • B. Tell coworkers
  • C. Access the chart only if required for legitimate care responsibilities
  • D. Post anonymously online

Rationale: Curiosity is never a legitimate reason to access a chart.

Practice 7 — Restraints

An older confused client repeatedly attempts to get out of bed. What should the nurse consider first?

  • A. Apply restraints immediately
  • B. Use appropriate less restrictive safety interventions
  • C. Sedate the client without an order
  • D. Tie the client to the bed

Rationale: Least restrictive effective option first.

Practice 8 — DNR

A client with a DNR order develops severe pain. Which action is appropriate?

  • A. Withhold pain medication because the client is DNR
  • B. Provide appropriate pain management
  • C. Stop all care
  • D. Remove oxygen regardless of need

Rationale: DNR does not mean do not treat.

Practice 9 — Abuse

The nurse suspects that a child may be experiencing abuse. Which action is appropriate?

  • A. Wait until abuse is proven beyond doubt
  • B. Personally interrogate the caregiver
  • C. Follow mandatory-reporting requirements
  • D. Ignore the findings to maintain confidentiality

Rationale: Reasonable suspicion triggers mandatory-reporting duties — the nurse does not need to prove abuse personally.

Practice 10 — Impaired Coworker

A nurse appears intoxicated while preparing medications. What is the priority?

  • A. Protect clients from unsafe care and follow reporting procedures
  • B. Help the nurse hide the impairment
  • C. Allow the nurse to finish the shift
  • D. Say nothing unless a client is injured

Rationale: Client safety comes first — never ignore or cover for impairment.

Practice 11 — Fall

A client is found on the floor. What should the nurse do first?

  • A. Immediately lift the client into bed
  • B. Assess the client for injury
  • C. Complete an incident report
  • D. Call housekeeping

Rationale: Always assess for injury before moving the client.

Practice 12 — Client Rights

A competent client says, "I want to leave the hospital." What should the nurse do first?

  • A. Lock the door
  • B. Restrain the client
  • C. Assess the client's reasons, understanding, and safety concerns
  • D. Tell the family to prevent the client from leaving

Rationale: Do not immediately remove a competent client's freedom — assess and follow appropriate procedures.

Practice 13 — Documentation

Which documentation is most appropriate?

  • A. "Client was rude and difficult."
  • B. "Client yelled, 'Leave me alone,' and pushed the meal tray away."
  • C. "Client has a terrible attitude."
  • D. "Client acted crazy."

Rationale: Use objective, observable facts — never labels or opinions.

Practice 14 — Advance Directive

A client with decision-making capacity states that they want treatment that differs from an older advance directive. Whose current decision should generally guide care?

  • A. The competent client's current decision
  • B. The old document regardless of current wishes
  • C. The nurse's preference
  • D. The family's preference

Rationale: A capable client's current decision governs over an older directive or surrogate.

Practice 15 — Delegation

Which task should remain with the RN?

  • A. Routine bathing of a stable client
  • B. Initial assessment of a newly admitted client
  • C. Routine daily weight
  • D. Ambulation of an already assessed stable client

Rationale: Initial assessment requires RN-level clinical judgment.

25. Decision Methods

Legal/Ethical Decision Method

  1. Does the client have capacity? → Respect autonomy.
  2. Is there immediate danger? → Protect safety.
  3. Is consent required? → Ensure informed consent.
  4. Is confidentiality involved? → Share only when permitted/required.
  5. Is an order unsafe? → Clarify before acting.
  6. Is the issue unresolved? → Use chain of command.

Safety Decision Method

  1. What could harm the client?
  2. Is harm occurring now?
  3. Can I stop the harm?
  4. Do I need to assess first?
  5. Who needs to be notified?
  6. What must happen before documentation?

27. Final Legal/Ethical Check
  • Is the client competent/capable of deciding? What does the client want?
  • Is informed consent complete? Is there an immediate safety threat?
  • Is confidentiality involved? Is reporting legally required?
  • Is the prescription safe? Can the nurse intervene first?
  • Does this require chain of command? Is this within nursing scope?
  • Is documentation objective? Is the least restrictive safe option being used?
The 10 Rules to Never Forget
  1. A competent client controls their healthcare decisions.
  2. The client may refuse treatment.
  3. Do not perform nonemergency treatment without appropriate consent.
  4. Protect confidentiality.
  5. Mandatory reporting may override confidentiality when required by law.
  6. Question unsafe prescriptions.
  7. If a safety concern remains unresolved, use chain of command.
  8. Assess/protect the client before completing incident paperwork.
  9. Use the least restrictive effective safety intervention.
  10. Document facts, not opinions.
Final Rule: When a legal, ethical, or safety question feels confusing, return to three questions — What does the client want? What keeps the client safe? What is the nurse legally and professionally allowed and required to do?

Client Rights + Client Safety + Nursing Accountability solve a large number of NCLEX legal, ethical, management-of-care, and safety questions.
