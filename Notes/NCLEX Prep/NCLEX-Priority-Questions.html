NCLEX Priority Questions: Who Comes First?

Priority questions are some of the most important questions on the NCLEX-RN — the goal is not to identify who is sick, but who is most unstable, at greatest immediate risk, and cannot safely wait.

Core Concept: In many priority questions, more than one answer may be correct nursing care. The challenge is deciding which problem is most urgent right now, or which action should happen first.
First Rule: The goal is not simply to identify who is sick. The goal is to identify who is most unstable, who is at greatest immediate risk, and what cannot safely wait.
1. What Is a Priority Question?

Trigger Words

Priority questions often include words such as:

  • First
  • Priority
  • Immediate
  • Most important
  • Highest priority
  • Assess first
  • See first
  • Intervene first
  • Report first
  • Take action first
Whenever one of these words appears, mentally translate the question into: What cannot safely wait?
2. Priority Does Not Mean "Who Looks the Sickest"

A client can have a serious disease and still be stable. Another client may have a less serious diagnosis but suddenly develop a dangerous change.

Client A

Chronic heart failure with bilateral ankle edema present for several weeks.

Client B — Priority

No respiratory distress earlier, suddenly develops severe shortness of breath and SpO₂ 82%.

Client B is the priority because it is a new, acute, unstable change. The diagnosis alone does not determine priority.
3. The Basic Priority Hierarchy

When comparing clients, think in this general order:

  • Unstable → Stable
  • Acute → Chronic
  • Unexpected → Expected
  • New change → Baseline finding
  • Life-threatening → Non-life-threatening
  • Actual problem → Potential problem
These rules are useful, but they are not absolute. Always evaluate the complete clinical situation.
4. Unstable vs. Stable

Unstable May Include

  • New respiratory distress
  • New chest pain
  • Sudden hypotension
  • New confusion
  • New neurological deficit
  • Active bleeding
  • Significant vital sign change
  • Sudden decrease in LOC
  • New seizure activity
  • Severe allergic reaction
  • Signs of shock
  • Airway compromise
  • Rapid deterioration

Stable May Include

  • Chronic symptoms
  • Expected postoperative findings
  • Predictable disease manifestations
  • Unchanged from baseline
  • Controlled pain
  • Stable vital signs
  • Improving symptoms
  • Long-standing abnormalities
NCLEX Rule: A newly unstable client usually takes priority over a chronically ill but stable client.
5. Acute vs. Chronic

Acute — New, Sudden, Rapidly Changing

  • Sudden chest pain
  • New shortness of breath
  • Sudden confusion
  • New weakness on one side
  • Sudden severe headache
  • New bleeding
  • Sudden decrease in urine output

Chronic — Long-Standing or Expected

  • Chronic back pain
  • Long-standing edema
  • Chronic COPD symptoms
  • Long-standing hypertension
  • Stable chronic kidney disease findings
Priority Rule: Acute changes usually take priority over chronic stable problems.
6. Expected vs. Unexpected

An expected finding usually has lower priority than an unexpected finding.

Expected

A postoperative client reports incisional pain rated 5/10.

Unexpected — Priority

Another postoperative client suddenly develops shortness of breath, restlessness, tachycardia, and SpO₂ 84%.

Rule: Unexpected findings often indicate a complication or deterioration.
7. New Change vs. Baseline

When the question gives previous information, compare before → now.

1BP 132/78 mm HgBP 82/46 mm HgMajor change — priority
2BP 90/60 mm Hg (baseline)BP 92/60 mm HgStable at baseline
Rule: The change is often more important than the number itself.
8. ABC — Airway, Breathing, Circulation

A — Airway

Can air move into the lungs?

  • Stridor
  • Airway obstruction
  • Choking
  • Severe facial/tongue swelling
  • Inability to speak
  • Severe secretions blocking airway
  • Loss of airway protective reflexes

B — Breathing

Is the client ventilating and oxygenating adequately?

  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Very low SpO₂ with symptoms
  • Cyanosis
  • Severe respiratory distress
  • Absent breath sounds
  • Extreme respiratory rate
  • Signs of respiratory failure

C — Circulation

Is blood reaching the organs?

  • Severe hypotension
  • Active major bleeding
  • Signs of shock
  • Severe dysrhythmia with instability
  • Poor perfusion
  • Sudden chest pain with hemodynamic changes
9. ABC Is Not Automatic

Do not simply see the word "airway" and choose it. ABC works best when there is an actual threat.

Client A

Chronic mild wheezing that is unchanged.

Client B — Priority

Suddenly develops crushing chest pain, diaphoresis, and BP 78/40 mm Hg.

Do not automatically choose Client A just because wheezing relates to breathing. Client B has an immediate circulation problem and is unstable. Use ABC together with the actual clinical picture.
10. Airway vs. Breathing

Airway Problem

Air cannot get through.

  • Choking
  • Stridor
  • Swollen tongue
  • Obstruction

Breathing Problem

Air can pass through, but ventilation/oxygenation is inadequate.

  • Severe asthma
  • Pulmonary edema
  • Pneumothorax
  • Respiratory failure
Airway obstruction is generally more immediately dangerous because without an open airway, breathing cannot occur.
11. Circulation Priorities

Circulation problems include more than blood pressure. Look for:

  • Massive bleeding
  • Severe hypotension
  • Weak pulses
  • Cool clammy skin
  • Altered mental status
  • Decreased urine output
  • Signs of shock
  • Unstable dysrhythmias

Example — Pattern of Poor Perfusion

BP 76/42 mm Hg, HR 132/min, cool clammy skin, confusion — much more concerning than a single mildly abnormal vital sign.

12. Neurological Changes Are High Priority

New neurological changes can indicate rapid deterioration:

  • New confusion
  • Sudden slurred speech
  • New unilateral weakness
  • Sudden decreased level of consciousness
  • New unequal pupils
  • New seizure activity
  • Sudden severe headache
Rule: A new change in mental or neurological status is never something to casually dismiss.
13. Actual vs. Potential Problems

In general, an actual problem takes priority over a potential problem.

Client A

At risk for falls (potential).

Client B — Priority

Actively bleeding from a surgical site (actual).

However, potential problems may become priority if the risk is immediately life-threatening — e.g. a client at immediate risk for airway obstruction following an allergic reaction. Actual > potential is a guide, not an absolute rule.
14. Life-Threatening vs. Non-Life-Threatening

Ask: Which problem could cause serious harm or death first?

Potentially Life-Threatening

  • Airway obstruction
  • Respiratory failure
  • Shock
  • Severe hemorrhage
  • Severe anaphylaxis
  • Unstable cardiac dysrhythmia
  • Acute neurological deterioration
  • Severe hypoglycemia with altered consciousness
  • Sepsis with instability

Compare With

  • Chronic pain
  • Constipation
  • Mild nausea
  • Routine discharge questions
  • Stable chronic findings
The life-threatening problem takes priority.
15. Maslow's Hierarchy

General order: Physiological needs → Safety → Love and belonging → Esteem → Self-actualization.

Physiological needs include oxygenation, circulation, nutrition, hydration, elimination, temperature, and sleep.

Client A

Anxious about a new diagnosis.

Client B — Priority

Severe hypoglycemia — threatens immediate survival.

16. Physical Needs Usually Come Before Psychosocial Needs

Client A

Crying because of a new cancer diagnosis.

Client B — Priority

New respiratory distress.

Both need nursing care, but the respiratory problem takes priority. This does not mean emotional concerns are unimportant — immediate physiological threats are treated before non-life-threatening psychosocial needs.
17. Safety Can Override Other Frameworks

If a client is in immediate danger, safety may become the priority:

  • Client actively attempting self-harm
  • Client trying to climb out of bed after sedation
  • Client receiving the wrong blood product
  • Client experiencing a severe transfusion reaction
  • Client with a medication causing anaphylaxis
The nurse must prevent immediate harm.
18–20. Assess First or Intervene First?

A common rule is to assess before implementing an intervention — but this is not always correct. Ask: Do I need more information, or do I already know enough to act?

Assess First — Usually When

  • Not immediately life-threatening
  • More information is needed
  • The cause is unclear
  • Client reports a new symptom but is stable
  • The intervention depends on assessment findings

Example: A stable client reports new abdominal pain — assess location, severity, characteristics, associated symptoms first.

Intervene First — Usually When

  • The emergency is obvious
  • Delay could harm the client
  • The nurse already has enough information
  • Immediate safety action is required

Example: A client is choking and unable to speak — act immediately, do not ask if they're having difficulty breathing.

21. Do Not Assess Something You Already Know

A client receiving an IV antibiotic develops wheezing, facial swelling, and difficulty breathing. The nurse already has enough information to recognize a severe allergic reaction — the priority is not a long additional assessment while the client deteriorates.

Rule: Assessment is important, but it should never unnecessarily delay emergency treatment.
22. Nursing Process and Priority

The nursing process is Assessment → Diagnosis/Analysis → Planning → Implementation → Evaluation. Assessment often comes before intervention — but if the client has an obvious life-threatening problem, intervention may be the priority.

23. "First Action" Questions

When asked which action the nurse should take first, ask:

  • Is the client stable?
  • Is there an immediate threat?
  • Do I need more information?
  • Is there something the nurse can do immediately?
  • Does one answer prevent harm before the others?
24. "Which Client Should the Nurse See First?"
  1. Look for airway problems.
  2. Look for breathing problems.
  3. Look for circulation problems.
  4. Look for acute neurological changes.
  5. Look for sudden or unexpected changes.
  6. Compare unstable vs. stable.
  7. Compare acute vs. chronic.
  8. Compare actual vs. potential problems.
  9. Consider safety.
  10. Ask who cannot safely wait.
25. Priority Red-Flag Words
  • Suddenly
  • New
  • Acute
  • Severe
  • Restless
  • Confused
  • Unresponsive
  • Stridor
  • Cyanosis
  • Dropping oxygen saturation
  • Hypotension
  • Active bleeding
  • Chest pressure
  • Facial swelling
  • Absent breath sounds
  • Slurred speech
  • Weakness
  • Seizure
  • Decreased urine output
These words often signal deterioration.
26–27. Pain Is Not Always the Priority — But Can Be a Red Flag

Client A

Postoperative pain 8/10, stable vital signs.

Client B — Priority

New confusion and SpO₂ 84%.

Severe pain does not automatically take priority over airway obstruction, respiratory distress, shock, active bleeding, or a new neurological deficit.
Pain becomes more concerning when it is sudden, severe, new, unexpected, different from usual, or associated with instability — e.g. sudden crushing chest pain, sudden tearing chest/back pain, sudden severe headache, or sudden severe abdominal pain with rigidity. Do not ignore pain merely because "pain is usually lower priority." Always consider context.
28. Don't Automatically Prioritize an Abnormal Lab Value

An abnormal lab value must be interpreted with severity, symptoms, clinical context, trend, and immediate risk. A mildly abnormal chronic value may be less urgent than a new respiratory problem — but a critical potassium level with ECG changes may become an immediate priority.

Rule: The number matters, but the client's condition matters more.
29–30. Don't Prioritize by Age or Diagnosis Alone

Age alone does not determine priority — do not automatically choose the newborn, the child, the pregnant client, or the older adult. Priority is based on instability, immediate risk, and clinical condition.

Client A

Myocardial infarction but currently stable after treatment.

Client B — Priority

Pneumonia, but suddenly becomes severely dyspneic and confused.

The diagnosis sounds less severe, but the client's current condition is worse.
31. First vs. Best vs. Most Important vs. Immediate
  • First — correct sequence.
  • Best — most appropriate among the available options.
  • Most important — highest significance or greatest risk.
  • Immediate — cannot safely wait.
Read the exact wording carefully.
32–34. Call the Provider or Act First?

A client receiving a blood transfusion develops chills, fever, and difficulty breathing. The nurse should not simply call the provider while the transfusion continues — the immediate action is to address the transfusion reaction (e.g. stop the transfusion per protocol), then notifications follow.

Rule: Do not delay an immediate nursing intervention just to notify someone.

Notify the provider when the nurse has completed necessary initial assessment, the client has a significant abnormal finding requiring medical management, the situation is not one the nurse can independently resolve, or new orders are needed.

A rapidly deteriorating client (severe respiratory distress, sudden major neurological change, severe hypotension, rapid deterioration, significant LOC change) may require escalation beyond a routine phone call.

Important Principle: The urgency of the response should match the urgency of the client's condition.
35–36. Priority After a Procedure or Medication

Expected

  • Mild incisional pain
  • Small amount of expected drainage
  • Mild postoperative fatigue

Concerning

  • Sudden dyspnea
  • Excessive bleeding
  • Stridor
  • Severe hypotension
  • New neurological deficit
  • Sudden loss of pulses distal to a vascular procedure
Unexpected complications take priority. After medication administration, ask whether a finding is an expected effect, side effect, or dangerous adverse reaction — difficulty breathing, facial swelling, severe hypotension, severe bleeding, dangerous dysrhythmias, altered consciousness, and severe hypoglycemia are high priority. Do not treat every mild side effect as an emergency.
37–39. Priority in Mental Health, Maternity, and Pediatrics

Mental Health

Highest priority: suicidal plan with means available, homicidal intent, severe agitation with immediate danger, command hallucinations instructing harmful behavior, and inability to maintain basic physiological needs.

Maternity

Look for acute maternal/fetal compromise: severe bleeding, seizure, severe hypertension with neurological symptoms, fetal heart rate deterioration, umbilical cord prolapse, signs of shock.

Pediatrics

Children can deteriorate rapidly — watch for airway problems, stridor, drooling with respiratory distress, cyanosis, severe retractions, altered consciousness, severe dehydration, poor perfusion, and seizure. Do not automatically prioritize a child simply because they are young; prioritize based on the clinical condition.

40. Priority in Postoperative Clients

Ask: What can kill the client first? Pay close attention to airway, breathing, bleeding, circulation, neurological changes, and significant change from baseline. Less urgent: mild incisional pain, mild nausea, routine questions, expected fatigue.

41–45. Quick Priority Tests
  • "Can this client wait?" — If I do not see this client for the next 10–15 minutes, who is most likely to deteriorate?
  • "What can kill first?" — Airway obstruction usually kills faster than constipation; respiratory failure usually kills faster than chronic pain; severe hemorrhage usually kills faster than a routine fever.
  • "New or old?" — Chronic atrial fibrillation with stable vital signs vs. new irregular rhythm with dizziness and BP 80/44 mm Hg → the new finding is the priority.
  • "Expected or unexpected?" — Expected incisional discomfort vs. new stridor after neck surgery → stridor is unexpected and threatens the airway.
  • "Stable or unstable?" — If one client is unstable (falling BP, dropping SpO₂, changing mental status, active bleeding, severe respiratory distress, new neurological deficits) and the others are stable, start with the unstable client.
46. Simple Priority Framework
  1. Airway — is the airway open?
  2. Breathing — can the client oxygenate and ventilate?
  3. Circulation — is perfusion adequate?
  4. Neurological status — any new change?
  5. Safety — is there immediate danger?
  6. Acute vs. chronic — which problem is new?
  7. Unexpected vs. expected — which finding suggests deterioration?
  8. Stable vs. unstable — who cannot wait?
Practice Walkthroughs

Practice 1 — Which Client Should Be Assessed First?

The nurse is caring for four clients. Which client should be assessed first?

  • A. A client with chronic back pain requesting prescribed pain medication
  • B. A client with COPD whose SpO₂ is 90%, consistent with baseline
  • C. A client who suddenly develops stridor after thyroid surgery
  • D. A client with diabetes requesting assistance with lunch

Rationale: Client C has a sudden change, a postoperative complication, and an airway threat. The other clients have needs, but they are more stable.

Practice 2 — Acute Change

Which client should the nurse see first?

  • A. A client with chronic heart failure and unchanged ankle edema
  • B. A client with pneumonia who suddenly becomes confused and has SpO₂ 84%
  • C. A client with arthritis reporting pain 6/10
  • D. A client waiting for discharge instructions

Rationale: Client B has an acute change, hypoxemia, a neurological change, and possible respiratory deterioration.

Practice 3 — Assess Before Intervening

A client reports new abdominal discomfort. The client is alert, vital signs are stable, and there is no bleeding. Which action should the nurse take first?

  • A. Assess the location and characteristics of the pain
  • B. Call a rapid response
  • C. Prepare the client for surgery
  • D. Administer an unprescribed analgesic

Rationale: The client is stable. More assessment is needed before determining the intervention.

Practice 4 — Obvious Emergency

A client suddenly becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally. Which action is the priority?

Strategy: This is not a situation in which the nurse should perform a lengthy assessment. Immediate emergency response is required.

Rule: Obvious emergency = act.

Practice 5 — Acute Neurological Emergency

Which client should the nurse assess first?

  • A. Client with chronic COPD and baseline SpO₂ 91%
  • B. Client with postoperative pain 7/10 and stable vital signs
  • C. Client with sudden unilateral weakness and slurred speech
  • D. Client requesting assistance to the bathroom

Rationale: Sudden unilateral weakness and slurred speech indicate an acute neurological emergency.

Practice 6 — Postoperative Priority

The nurse is caring for four postoperative clients. Which finding requires immediate attention?

  • A. Mild nausea after anesthesia
  • B. Incisional pain rated 5/10
  • C. Small amount of drainage on a dressing
  • D. Sudden dyspnea with oxygen saturation 83%

Rationale: The question asks for immediate attention. Respiratory compromise takes priority over expected postoperative findings.

Practice 7 — Anaphylaxis

A client receiving an IV antibiotic develops facial swelling, wheezing, and difficulty breathing. Which action is the priority?

Strategy: The client has signs of severe anaphylaxis — an airway/breathing emergency. Immediate intervention is required. Do not delay emergency action for routine documentation or teaching.

Practice 8 — Active Bleeding

Which client should be assessed first?

  • A. Client with stable chronic kidney disease and creatinine at baseline
  • B. Client with diabetes whose glucose is mildly elevated before lunch
  • C. Client with active gastrointestinal bleeding and BP 82/46 mm Hg
  • D. Client requesting a sleeping medication

Rationale: Client C has active bleeding, severe hypotension, and circulation compromise.

Practice 9 — Expected Improvement vs. New Change

A nurse is caring for a client who received pain medication 30 minutes ago and now reports pain decreased from 8/10 to 4/10. Another client suddenly develops new confusion. Who is the priority?

Answer: The client with new confusion. The first client is showing expected improvement; the second has an unexpected neurological change.

Practice 10 — Immediate Safety Threat

Which client should be seen first?

  • A. A client with depression who reports poor sleep
  • B. A client with anxiety who requests medication
  • C. A client stating, "I have a plan to kill myself tonight."
  • D. A client who is upset about being hospitalized

Rationale: An immediate safety threat takes priority.

Final Two Priority Answers

When two answers both seem correct, ask:

  • Which client is more unstable?
  • Which problem is newer?
  • Which finding is unexpected?
  • Which client has an actual airway, breathing, or circulation threat?
  • Which client is deteriorating?
  • Which problem can cause harm fastest?
  • Which client cannot safely wait?
  • Is one answer based on a chronic baseline?
  • Is one finding expected?
  • Am I prioritizing the diagnosis instead of the current condition?

  • U — Unstable. Who is unstable?
  • A — Acute. What is new?
  • U — Unexpected. What should not be happening?

Question Error Analysis

When a priority question is missed, determine why:

  • Misread "first" or "priority"
  • Used ABC automatically
  • Ignored acute change or baseline
  • Chose chronic over acute, or expected over unexpected
  • Chose pain over instability
  • Called provider before nursing action
  • Assessed during an obvious emergency
  • Missed a neurological change
  • Prioritized diagnosis instead of current condition

Final Priority Check

Before answering a priority question, ask:

  1. Is anyone unstable?
  2. Is there an airway threat?
  3. Is there a breathing problem?
  4. Is there a circulation problem?
  5. Is there a new neurological change?
  6. Is this acute or chronic?
  7. Is this expected or unexpected?
  8. Is this a new change from baseline?
  9. Is there an immediate safety threat?
  10. Who cannot safely wait?
Key Rules to Remember
  • Unstable usually comes before stable.
  • Acute usually comes before chronic.
  • Unexpected usually comes before expected.
  • A new change usually comes before a baseline finding.
  • Life-threatening problems come before routine needs.
  • ABC is important, but do not use it blindly.
  • An actual airway threat is different from simply having a respiratory diagnosis.
  • New neurological changes are high priority.
  • Do not automatically prioritize pain.
  • Do not prioritize based on diagnosis alone.
  • Do not choose the most abnormal number without considering the client's condition.
  • Assess first when more information is needed; act first when the emergency is obvious and delay is dangerous.
  • Do not call the provider before performing an immediate nursing intervention already within your role.
  • Ask who cannot safely wait.
Final Rule: Priority is not about who has the worst disease. It is about who is most likely to deteriorate if the nurse does not act now. Ask: Who is unstable? What changed? Is there an ABC threat? Is this expected or unexpected? Who cannot safely wait?
