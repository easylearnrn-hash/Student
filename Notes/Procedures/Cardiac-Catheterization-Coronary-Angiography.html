Cardiac Catheterization / Coronary Angiography

Invasive imaging of the coronary arteries — indications, pre/post care, complications, and NCLEX priorities

What It Is & Why It Is Done

Overview

  • Cardiac catheterization is an invasive procedure in which a thin catheter is inserted through a blood vessel and advanced to the heart
  • Coronary angiography uses iodinated contrast (radiopaque dye visible on X-ray) during cardiac catheterization to visualize the coronary arteries

Used To

  • Identify coronary artery narrowing or blockage
  • Evaluate chest pain or suspected CAD (coronary artery disease)
  • Measure pressures inside the heart
  • Evaluate valves and cardiac function
  • Determine need for PCI (percutaneous coronary intervention — opening a narrowed coronary artery with balloon angioplasty and/or stent) or surgery
How It Is Performed
1
The patient receives local anesthesia and usually moderate sedation.
2
A catheter is inserted through the radial artery in the wrist, or the femoral artery in the groin.
3
The catheter is advanced to the heart using fluoroscopy (continuous X-ray imaging).
4
Contrast is injected into the coronary arteries.
5
X-ray images identify narrowing or obstruction.
6
PCI may be performed immediately if significant blockage is found.
7
The catheter is removed and bleeding is controlled with manual pressure or a vascular closure device.
Pre-Procedure Nursing Care & Patient Teaching

Baseline & Labs

  • Verify informed consent
  • Baseline vital signs, ECG, peripheral pulses, skin color/temperature/capillary refill
  • Review CBC, electrolytes, kidney function (creatinine, eGFR), coagulation studies
  • Assess for previous reaction to iodinated contrast

Prep & Teaching

  • Review anticoagulants, antiplatelets, and diabetes medications
  • Follow provider instructions regarding metformin — contrast may require temporary interruption in at-risk patients
  • Maintain NPO status as ordered before sedation
  • Establish IV access
  • Teach: warmth/flushing may occur with contrast injection, remain still, mild pressure at insertion site may occur
NCLEX Trap: A shellfish allergy alone does not mean the patient is allergic to iodinated contrast.
Nurse's Role During the Procedure
  • Maintain sterile technique
  • Monitor continuously: ECG/rhythm, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, level of consciousness
  • Observe for dysrhythmias, chest pain, hypotension, contrast reaction, respiratory difficulty
  • Administer medications, sedation, anticoagulants, or antiplatelet drugs as ordered
  • Be prepared for emergency resuscitation if serious dysrhythmias occur
Post-Procedure Nursing Care & Monitoring
Priority: Bleeding + circulation + cardiac status.
  • Monitor vital signs and cardiac rhythm
  • Assess catheter insertion site frequently for bleeding, hematoma (collection of blood under the skin), swelling
  • Check distal circulation: pulses, color, temperature, capillary refill, sensation
  • Compare affected extremity with the opposite extremity

Femoral Approach

  • Keep affected leg straight as ordered
  • Maintain bed rest according to access-site management protocol

Radial Approach

  • Monitor hand circulation and radial pulse
  • Avoid excessive wrist movement

Encourage fluids when appropriate to promote contrast elimination and monitor urine output/kidney function.

Expected vs. Abnormal Findings

✅ Expected

  • Mild soreness or small bruise at insertion site
  • Warm/flushed sensation during contrast injection
  • Stable vital signs
  • Palpable distal pulses
  • No active bleeding

⚠️ Abnormal — Report Immediately

  • Active or increasing bleeding
  • Rapidly enlarging hematoma
  • Weak/absent distal pulse
  • Cool, pale, numb, or painful extremity
  • Chest pain
  • Severe hypotension
  • Shortness of breath
  • New dysrhythmia
  • Decreased urine output
  • Severe back/flank pain after femoral catheterization
Major Complications & Nursing Actions
ComplicationNursing Action
Hemorrhage / HematomaApply firm direct pressure immediately, keep patient in appropriate position, assess vital signs, notify provider
Retroperitoneal hemorrhage (internal bleeding behind the abdominal cavity — especially after femoral access)Signs: hypotension, tachycardia, back/flank/abdominal/groin pain, falling H&H → notify provider/emergency team immediately
Arterial occlusion / impaired circulationSigns: cool or pale extremity, pain/numbness, weak or absent pulse → notify provider immediately
Contrast-associated acute kidney injuryMonitor creatinine, urine output, and hydration status
Contrast hypersensitivity reactionWatch for hives, wheezing, facial swelling, hypotension → stop exposure if possible, maintain airway, initiate emergency treatment
Dysrhythmias / myocardial ischemiaContinuous ECG monitoring; report significant rhythm changes or chest pain
Post-Procedure Patient Teaching
  • Keep insertion site clean and dry as instructed
  • Avoid heavy lifting/strenuous activity for the prescribed period
  • Increase fluids if not contraindicated
  • Report immediately: persistent bleeding, large/increasing swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness/fainting, coolness/numbness/color change of the affected limb, fever or drainage
  • If bleeding starts at home → lie down and apply firm direct pressure; seek emergency help if bleeding does not stop
Contraindications / Important Precautions
  • Severe kidney impairment
  • Previous severe contrast reaction
  • Active bleeding
  • Uncontrolled anticoagulation
  • Severe anemia
  • Uncontrolled hypertension
  • Significant electrolyte abnormalities
  • Active infection
  • Hemodynamic instability unless catheterization is being performed emergently
NCLEX Priority Points & Common Test Traps
High-Yield Points:
  • Post-cath priority = insertion site + distal pulses + vital signs
  • Bleeding from catheter site → direct pressure first
  • Femoral access → keep leg straight as ordered
  • Back/flank pain + hypotension after femoral cath → suspect retroperitoneal bleeding
  • Cool/pale extremity + weak pulse → suspect arterial occlusion
  • Encourage hydration when appropriate to help clear contrast
  • Monitor kidney function after iodinated contrast, especially in high-risk patients
  • Warm flushing during contrast injection can be expected
  • Hives, wheezing, swelling, or hypotension are NOT expected → suspect hypersensitivity reaction
  • Shellfish allergy ≠ automatic iodinated contrast allergy
🧠 NCLEX Memory Line: CARDIAC CATH → contrast + coronary imaging → monitor ECG → check site + pulses → bleeding = pressure → femoral back pain + hypotension = retroperitoneal bleed → protect kidneys.
