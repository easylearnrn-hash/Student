Burns and Skin

Pressure Ulcers (Bedsores)

Prevention, staging, and treatment of pressure injuries

What Is a Pressure Ulcer?

A pressure ulcer (bedsore, decubitus ulcer) is skin damage caused by prolonged pressure on the skin. This pressure reduces blood flow, leading to skin breakdown and open wounds. Common in patients who are bedridden, immobile, or have poor circulation.

Causes & Risk Factors

  • Prolonged pressure — lying or sitting in one position too long
  • Friction — rubbing against a surface (e.g., when moving in bed)
  • Shearing — skin slides one direction while the body moves another (e.g., sliding down in bed)
  • Moisture — sweat, urine, or stool weakens skin (common in incontinent patients)
  • Poor nutrition & hydration — lack of protein, vitamins, water weakens skin
  • Medical conditions — diabetes, poor circulation, spinal cord injuries increase risk

Common Locations (Bony Prominences)

Back of head (occiput), shoulders & shoulder blades, elbows, lower back & tailbone (sacrum & coccyx), hips & buttocks, heels & ankles.

Prevention (Best Strategy!)

  • Reposition every 2 hours — helps relieve pressure
  • Use pillows or foam pads to support bony areas
  • Keep skin clean & dry — prevents breakdown from moisture
  • Apply barrier creams (zinc oxide, petroleum jelly) against urine & stool
  • Encourage a high-protein diet — protein helps heal skin
  • Use special mattresses (low-air-loss or foam) to reduce pressure
  • Assess skin daily, especially on bony areas

Treatment

Stage 1 & 2 — Mild to Moderate
  • Keep the area clean & dry
  • Apply moisture-retaining dressings (hydrocolloid, transparent film)
  • Encourage movement & repositioning
Stage 3 & 4 — Severe, Deep Wounds
  • Debridement — surgical or with special dressings
  • Dressings: hydrocolloid (moderate drainage), foam (heavy drainage), alginate (absorbs excess drainage)
  • Antibiotic ointment if infected
  • Negative pressure wound therapy (wound VAC)
  • May need skin grafting for large ulcers

Dressing Types for Pressure Injury Care

For NCLEX-RN pressure injury care, focus on these dressing types:

Dressing TypeBest UseKey NCLEX Point
Transparent film
Superficial wounds with little or no drainageDoes not absorb much drainage. Avoid with heavily draining or infected wounds.
Hydrocolloid
Shallow wounds with light-to-moderate drainageMaintains a moist environment and promotes autolytic debridement. Usually avoid in heavily infected or heavily draining wounds.
Hydrogel
Dry wounds or wounds with dry slough/escharAdds moisture. Do not use for heavily draining wounds.
Foam dressing
Moderate drainage; Stage 2–4 woundsAbsorbs drainage and provides cushioning/protection.
Alginate
Heavy drainage, deep wounds, cavities, minor bleedingHighly absorbent. Never use on a dry wound. Often needs a secondary dressing.
Hydrofiber
Moderate-to-heavy drainage, deep or cavity woundsAbsorbs drainage and forms a gel. Avoid on completely dry wounds.
Gauze / packing
Deep wounds, tunnels, cavitiesPack loosely, never tightly. Plain dry gauze may damage healthy tissue when removed.
Antimicrobial dressing
Infected wounds or high bacterial burdenExamples include silver-containing dressings. Not needed for every pressure injury.
Negative-pressure wound therapy (wound VAC)
Selected deep Stage 3–4 pressure injuriesUses suction to remove drainage and promote granulation tissue. Check the seal and suction system.
Skin barrier products
Protect skin around the wound, especially with incontinenceExamples: zinc oxide and barrier film. Prevents moisture-associated skin damage.

Highest-Yield NCLEX Rules

  • Dry wound → Hydrogel
  • Heavy drainage → Alginate or Hydrofiber
  • Moderate drainage → Foam
  • Minimal drainage/superficial → Transparent film or Hydrocolloid
  • Deep cavity → Loosely pack
  • Infection → Antimicrobial dressing if indicated
  • Stage 1 → Pressure relief/offloading is the priority
  • Stable, dry, intact heel eschar → Do NOT debride
  • Never tightly pack a pressure ulcer
  • Never use alginate on a dry wound
  • Pressure relief and repositioning are essential regardless of the dressing used.

Complications

⚠️
Infection (Cellulitis, Osteomyelitis, Sepsis)

Redness, swelling, warmth, pus = infection. Fever, chills, confusion = sepsis (life-threatening). Bone infection (osteomyelitis) can develop in Stage 4 ulcers.

⚠️
Delayed Healing & Mobility Issues

Poor nutrition (low protein, vitamin deficiencies) slows healing. Diabetes & poor circulation increase risk of non-healing ulcers. Pain from deep ulcers can limit movement; contractures can develop if patients don't move.

NCLEX Priorities

  • Turn & reposition every 2 hours — prevention is key
  • Keep skin dry but moisturized — moisture balance matters
  • Use special mattresses & pillows to reduce pressure
  • Encourage a high-protein, high-calorie diet for healing
  • Monitor for signs of infection (fever, redness, pus, worsening pain)
📝
NCLEX "Must-Know" Concepts

Stage 1 = red, intact skin, non-blanchable → turn every 2 hours. Stage 2 = open blister/shallow wound → moist dressings help heal. Stage 3 = deep wound, fat visible → needs special wound care. Stage 4 = muscle/bone exposed → may need surgery. Unstageable = needs debridement to assess depth. Deep Tissue Injury = purple/maroon skin = damage underneath. Repositioning every 2 hours is the best prevention. High-protein diet speeds healing. Fever + worsening ulcer = possible sepsis — call the provider.
