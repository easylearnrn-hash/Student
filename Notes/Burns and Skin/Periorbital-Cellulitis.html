Burns and Skin

Periorbital Cellulitis

Preseptal infection vs. sight-threatening orbital cellulitis

Definition

Periorbital cellulitis (also called preseptal cellulitis) is an infection of the eyelid and surrounding skin tissues in front of the orbital septum — the connective tissue barrier separating the front of the eye from the deeper orbital structures.

📌
Good News

It's a serious but usually non-vision-threatening condition if treated promptly.

Do Not Confuse With: Orbital Cellulitis

FeaturePeriorbital CellulitisOrbital Cellulitis
LocationAnterior to (in front of) orbital septumPosterior to (behind) the septum — within the orbit
Frequency/SeverityMore common, less dangerousRare, but more severe & sight-threatening
Eye Movement Pain❌ Absent✅ Present
Vision Changes❌ None✅ Possible
Proptosis (Bulging Eye)❌ No✅ Yes
UrgencyUrgent, but outpatient possibleEmergency — IV antibiotics/hospitalization
Common CauseSkin trauma, sinusitisSinusitis, progression from periorbital cellulitis

Causes / Risk Factors

Bacterial infection, often from:

  • Sinusitis (especially ethmoid sinus)
  • Eyelid trauma (insect bite, scratch, injury)
  • Chalazion or stye infection
  • Spread from upper respiratory tract infection
Common Organisms

Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae (in unvaccinated children)

Signs & Symptoms

  • Eyelid swelling, redness, and tenderness
  • Warmth over the eyelid
  • Fever may be present
  • No pain with eye movement
  • No vision changes
  • Eye movements and pupil responses are normal
  • May have history of insect bite, sinus infection, or trauma

Diagnosis

  • Physical exam — distinguish from orbital cellulitis (deeper involvement)
  • CT scan of orbits with contrast — if uncertain or worsening; rules out orbital cellulitis or abscess
  • CBC — may show elevated WBC; blood cultures if febrile or toxic-appearing

Treatment

SeverityTreatment
Mild (outpatient)Oral antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate)
Moderate/severe or <1 year oldIV antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone, vancomycin) in hospital
Complications or no improvementMay need surgical drainage or ophthalmology referral

Nursing Considerations

  • Monitor temperature and vital signs regularly
  • Assess for progression to orbital cellulitis: pain with eye movement, bulging eye (proptosis), double vision (diplopia), decreased visual acuity
  • Administer antibiotics on time (oral or IV as prescribed)
  • Apply warm compresses to the eyelid if approved by provider
  • Encourage medication compliance at home to prevent complications
  • Educate caregivers to return if symptoms worsen

NCLEX Practice Questions

Q1

A 6-year-old child is brought in with eyelid swelling, redness, and warmth but no eye pain with movement and normal vision. What is the most likely diagnosis?

✅ Periorbital (preseptal) cellulitis — classic signs of infection in front of the orbital septum, with no deeper (orbital) involvement.

Q2

Which finding would cause the nurse to suspect orbital cellulitis instead of periorbital cellulitis?

✅ Pain with eye movement — suggests deeper infection behind the septum → possible orbital cellulitis.

Q3

Which teaching point is most important for parents of a child discharged with periorbital cellulitis?

✅ "Call your provider if your child's eye becomes difficult to open or moves painfully." Worsening symptoms may mean progression to orbital cellulitis and need for urgent care.

📝
Final NCLEX Tips

Most common in children under 10. Differentiate from orbital cellulitis by checking eye movement pain and vision. Prompt antibiotic treatment is key. Always educate caregivers on red-flag symptoms: eye pain, vision loss, fever. A CT scan may be ordered to rule out deeper infection.
